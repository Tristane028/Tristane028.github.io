About

Who I Am

I am Tristan Endo (遠藤俊治), a fourth-year Math-CS student at UC San Diego. To be honest, math never really clicked for me until I could watch it actually do something. So I ended up chasing the parts that solve real problems instead of the ones that just sit on paper, and that is pretty much how I drifted into data science and finance.

In practice, that has meant a little bit of everything: writing code to poke at how neural networks behave, simulating Brownian motion, and tearing audio tracks apart by frequency to see what is actually inside them. From January to August 2026, I was a Quantitative Research & AI Engineering intern at Tekly Studio. There, I finished the full ML track and built an XGBoost momentum strategy for crypto markets, tested with purged cross-validation and walk-forward backtesting so the model never got to peek at the future. Right now, most of my time goes to research with Prof. Raymond Chou on Springer fibers.

The question I keep circling back to is how we can tell when a model is wrong. Lately, that has pulled me toward NLP and AI hallucination. My hunch is that if you look closely enough at the patterns and sentence structure in what a model says, you can start catching the inconsistencies before they snowball. That feels like a problem actually worth chasing.

01
Springer Fibers: Algebraic Geometry & Combinatorics

Ongoing research with Prof. Raymond Chou at UCSD, with Alexander Ryan. I built a Python/SymPy pipeline that takes a Jordan type, builds every cell of its Springer fiber with Plücker coordinates, and searches for witness curves showing which cells each closure meets. It now covers 99 Jordan types with two, three, and four rows (25,588 cells and 709,116 relations), well past the two-row cases where the existing theory gives answers. Along the way I found that a clean-looking result can still hide gaps: an exact check of (3,2,2) confirmed every relation the search found, and also turned up intersections it had missed in 68 of 210 closures. Try the explorer in the Simulation section below.

02
Neural Network Gaussian Process & Conjugate Kernels

Course project in Prof. Alexander Cloninger's MATH 216B at UCSD. In the infinite-width limit, a random network turns into a Gaussian process, and you can write down exactly how the correlation between two inputs changes layer by layer. I worked out how ReLU, erf, and tanh behave: with depth, ReLU drives every pair of inputs toward correlation 1 (degeneration), while erf and tanh push them toward a fixed point below 1 (decorrelation). Then I coded finite-width networks to test how well those predictions hold in practice. The live version is in the Simulation section below.

Neural Network Gaussian Processes: Theory vs. Real Networks

This is the experiment from my MATH 216B course project with Prof. Cloninger, rebuilt to run live. Two inputs with correlation ρ0 go through a random network. As the width goes to infinity, their correlation at every layer is given exactly by the NNGP kernel recursion (gold line). The blue line is the average over real, finite-width random networks, and the band shows how much individual networks scatter around it. At the default settings, ReLU drives the correlation toward 1 with depth, so every input starts to look the same (degeneration), while erf and tanh push it toward a fixed point below 1 (decorrelation).

Width sweep: how far real networks sit from the theory

The right panel shows one step of the recursion, cl+1 = f(cl), at the limiting variance q*, with the staircase tracing where the correlation goes layer by layer. Its slope at c = 1 is χ1: below 1 the network is in the ordered phase and inputs collapse together, above 1 it is chaotic and they spread apart. ReLU with σw² = 2 sits exactly at χ1 = 1, which is why its correlation creeps toward 1 so slowly. The width sweep measures the RMS gap between real networks and the theory across all layers, against a dashed 1/√width reference.

Springer Fiber Closure Explorer

A compact, flat version of the full 3D viewer from my research with Prof. Chou, running on real data from the project. Each dot is a cell of the Springer fiber, stacked by dimension. Click a cell to light up every cell its closure meets. Click one of those to look from inside both closures at once, and keep nesting. Hover a lit cell to see the witness curve that proves the relation. With one cell selected, play its halving route: pick a cell, keep only the part of the closure inside it, and land on a single cell, shrinking by a power of 2 each step.

An arrow from A to B means the closure of cell A meets cell B. The witness is a one-parameter family of points in A whose limit lands in B. These relations do not compose: if A's closure meets B and B's closure meets C, A's closure need not meet C. Across the whole project the search has found closure data for 99 Jordan types (25,588 cells and 709,116 relations), and every cell with more than one cell in its closure has a halving route.
